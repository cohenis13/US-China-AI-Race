Methodology

How the U.S.–China AI Race Tracker measures each dimension — sources, proxies, and known limitations.

Overview Executive Summary — Score Construction

What this is

The executive summary section at the top of the dashboard aggregates the six dimension outputs into a single comparative scorecard. It is generated automatically by scripts/build_executive_summary.py and stored in data/executive_summary.json, which the frontend loads on every page view. The builder runs as the final step of every dimension refresh workflow, so the summary always reflects the most recent data across all six dimensions.

Normalization approach

Dimensions use one of three methods to produce a 0–10 score:

Count-share (Frontier Models, Talent, Compute)

For Frontier Models, Talent, and Compute, each dimension produces a composite score that already sums to 100 across US+China (share-of-combined). This is then scaled to 0–10 and clamped to [0.5, 9.5]: 

us_score  = clamp( (US / (US + China)) × 10, 0.5, 9.5 )
china_score = 10.0 − us_score

By construction, U.S. + China = 10 for these dimensions.

Independent composite / 10 (Adoption, Energy)

Adoption and Energy use 0–100 composite scores (computed independently for each country). The executive summary score is simply: 

score = composite_score / 10

These scores do not sum to 10 — they are independent assessments of each country’s standing on the underlying proxies.

Share-of-combined / 10 (Diffusion)

Diffusion composite scores already sum to 100 (U.S. + China = 100 by construction). The executive summary score is: 

score = composite_score / 10

So U.S. + China ≈ 10 for Diffusion (small rounding differences only).

Score table and radar chart

The six scores populate both the radar chart and the dimension score table. The radar chart order is fixed as: Frontier Models, Compute, Adoption, Diffusion, Energy, Talent — matching the DIMS array in index.html.

Text generation

The “Current read” sentence and four strategic insight bullets are generated programmatically from the scored dimensions. The current read lists dimensions by winner (U.S. vs. China), sorted by margin (largest advantage first). The insights follow a fixed template: (1) strongest U.S. advantage; (2) strongest China advantage; (3) most closely contested dimension by gap; (4) a standing note on binding structural constraints.

Key caveats

  • Compute score (~7.6 US / ~2.4 China): Triangulated lower bound. China’s true share is likely 25–40% — the scored composite (~25%) reflects systematic non-reporting, not actual compute absence. The separate hidden-compute band in the UI documents this explicitly.
  • Frontier Models score: Reflects open model ecosystem (HuggingFace + ModelScope supplement). Closed-model capability (GPT-4o, Claude, Qwen API, Doubao) is not captured.
  • Talent score (~5.0/5.0 near-tie): Composite of research output, PhD pipeline, and elite researcher placement. China leads heavily on pipeline and output volume; the US leads on elite lab concentration. The near-tie reflects a real structural tension — if China-to-US talent flows changed materially, China's score would rise.
  • Adoption and Energy scores: Based on curated reference data with annual update cadence — not live signals.

Adoption AI Adoption in the Economy — Multi-Proxy Index

What this measures

A composite index approximating economy-wide AI adoption in the U.S. and China, built from two public proxies: enterprise AI adoption rates (survey-based) and industrial automation density (robot density per manufacturing worker). The composite is a transparent, directional proxy — not a definitive measure of total national AI usage.

Adoption vs. diffusion: This section focuses on adoption — whether and how much firms and institutions are using AI. It does not attempt to fully measure diffusion (how broadly that use spreads across geographies and sectors), which is a distinct and harder-to-measure concept.

Why a multi-proxy composite index?

No single public data source provides a clean, symmetric, and automatable measure of AI adoption across both the U.S. and Chinese economies:

  • Survey data (e.g., McKinsey) has limited China-specific granularity and inconsistent sampling across countries. It is more useful as a directional signal than a precise national figure.
  • Public filing data (e.g., SEC EDGAR) covers Chinese ADRs only — a technology-heavy sample that excludes Tencent, ByteDance, Huawei, and the vast majority of Chinese firms. This created a structurally asymmetric comparison.
  • Hard deployment metrics (e.g., industrial robot density) are highly symmetric and verifiable but measure industrial automation broadly, not AI specifically.

The composite combines these proxies so that their individual weaknesses are partially offset. Weights and normalization are fully documented and transparent.

Proxy 1 — Enterprise AI Adoption Rate (55% weight)

Sources: McKinsey & Company, The State of AI 2025 (published May 2025), and Stanford HAI AI Index 2025 (published April 2025). Supplemented by CAICT (China Academy of Information and Communications Technology) White Paper on China’s AI Development (2024) for the China-specific figure.

Definition: Percentage of organizations reporting that they use AI in at least one business function.

Values used: U.S. (North America): ~72% (McKinsey 2025, corroborated by Stanford AI Index 2025). China: ~70% (estimated from McKinsey 2025 regional data, CAICT 2024, and Stanford AI Index 2025; see coverage note below).

Normalization: Already expressed as a percentage; used directly on the 0–100 scale.

Coverage note — China: The McKinsey survey does not cleanly break out China at the country level. The ~68% figure is an estimate derived from McKinsey’s 2024 global and regional data, cross-referenced with CAICT surveys on Chinese enterprise AI adoption. It should be treated as directional (confidence: medium), not as a precise national figure.

Proxy 2 — Industrial Robot Density (45% weight)

Source: International Federation of Robotics (IFR), World Robotics 2024 annual report (published October 2024, covering 2023 operational data).

Definition: Installed industrial robots per 10,000 manufacturing workers. This is one of the most publicly verifiable and country-comparable measures of automation deployment depth embedded in the physical economy.

Values used: U.S.: 295 robots/10K workers (up from 274 in 2022). China: 470 robots/10K workers (up from 392 in 2022 — China overtook Japan and Germany in density ranking in 2023). For reference: South Korea ~1,012 (global outlier); Germany ~429; Japan ~419.

Normalization: (value ÷ 600) × 100, where 600 robots/10K workers is the normalization reference — above the leading non-outlier economies (Germany ~429, Japan ~419, China ~470) and well below South Korea’s ~1,012. The reference was updated from 500 to 600 when China’s 2023 value (470) crowded the previous ceiling.

Scope note: Robot density measures industrial automation broadly, not AI-specific deployment. It captures physical automation in manufacturing (automotive, electronics, precision assembly) and is an imperfect proxy for AI adoption in services, software, or consumer applications. Its strength is its symmetry and verifiability; its limitation is that it conflates pre-AI robotics with AI-driven automation.

Composite construction

Formula: Composite = 0.55 × enterprise_adoption_score + 0.45 × robot_density_norm_score

If a proxy is unavailable for one country, that proxy is excluded and the remaining proxy is re-weighted to 100% for that country. This prevents a missing proxy from artificially zeroing out one country’s score.

Enterprise adoption is weighted slightly higher (55%) because it more directly measures AI-specific organizational behavior. Robot density (45%) is a more objective and symmetric measure but captures industrial automation broadly.

Update cadence and automation

The script runs weekly via GitHub Actions. The underlying reference data updates annually: McKinsey publishes in approximately May each year; Stanford AI Index in approximately April; IFR publishes in approximately October. When new editions are released, the reference values in scripts/fetch_adoption.py are updated manually and the composite recalculates automatically on the next run.

Next expected updates: IFR World Robotics 2025 (~October 2025, covering 2024 data); McKinsey State of AI 2026 (~May 2026); Stanford AI Index 2026 (~April 2026).

What this composite does NOT capture

  • Consumer AI usage (individuals using AI tools, apps, or devices)
  • AI usage by small and medium enterprises
  • Private or unreported AI deployment
  • AI application quality or depth of integration
  • Software-only AI deployments not captured in industrial robot density
  • Sector-specific adoption in financial services, healthcare, or consumer services
  • The vast majority of Chinese firms (domestic, non-export-listed companies)

Why the previous approach (SEC EDGAR filing disclosure) was replaced

The v1 adoption section used SEC EDGAR full-text search to classify companies based on AI disclosure language in annual filings (10-K for US, 20-F for Chinese ADRs). This approach had a structural flaw: the China sample consisted exclusively of US-listed Chinese ADRs — a technology-heavy subset that excluded Tencent, ByteDance, Huawei, CATL, and most of China’s economy. The comparison was asymmetric by construction. The new composite index uses sources that are either genuinely country-comparable (IFR) or that, while imperfect, apply broadly to both economies (McKinsey global survey).

Diffusion Global AI Diffusion — Two-Proxy Composite

What this measures

How far U.S. and Chinese AI stacks have spread beyond their home markets — the external reach of each country’s AI ecosystem in terms of model adoption by foreign developers and cloud infrastructure accessible to foreign customers.

Diffusion vs. Adoption: This section focuses on diffusion — whether and how far a country’s AI outputs are being absorbed into foreign economies, products, and research pipelines. It does not measure domestic AI usage, which is tracked separately under Adoption.

Both proxies are scored as a share of the combined U.S. + China total, so the two country scores always sum to 100. This framing reflects the competitive nature of diffusion: reach that one country gains is, in relative terms, reach the other does not hold.

Proxy 1 — HF Open-Model Monthly Downloads (55% weight, live)

Source: Hugging Face Hub API (live, daily refresh). Labs and country mappings from data/labs.json — the same curated list used by the Frontier Models dimension.

Definition: Sum of monthly downloads across the top-100 most-downloaded models per tracked lab. Unlike the Frontier Models dimension, there is no date filter — this captures the full active install base, not just recent activity. A model being downloaded globally indicates active embedding in foreign products, pipelines, and research.

Scoring: (country downloads / US + China combined downloads) × 100.

Known undercount — China: Hugging Face Hub is the dominant platform for the global open-source developer community, but Chinese models are also widely distributed through ModelScope, Gitee AI, and other domestic platforms. Downloads via those platforms are not captured here. The China HF share is therefore a lower bound on total Chinese open-model diffusion.

Proxy 2 — Cloud AI Platform International Footprint (45% weight, curated)

Sources: Official provider documentation — AWS Global Infrastructure, Microsoft Azure geographies, Google Cloud locations, Alibaba Cloud global locations, Huawei Cloud regions, Tencent Cloud regions — as of Q1 2026.

Definition: Number of international countries and territories served by each country’s major public cloud providers with AI/ML platform services (combined, deduplicated estimate).

Values used: U.S. (AWS + Azure + Google Cloud): ~190 countries/territories. China (Alibaba Cloud + Huawei Cloud + Tencent Cloud): ~70 countries/territories.

Scoring: (country countries / US + China combined countries) × 100.

Why this proxy: Cloud footprint is the infrastructure layer of AI diffusion. A foreign organization that cannot access a country’s cloud provider cannot easily adopt that country’s AI APIs, services, or managed models. U.S. providers near-saturate the accessible global market; Chinese providers have expanded significantly through Belt and Road Initiative partner countries and enterprise markets in Asia-Pacific, Middle East, and Africa, but remain below U.S. coverage in aggregate.

Scope note: Country count is a measure of reach, not depth. A cloud provider that serves 190 countries may have vastly different revenue, usage, and AI workload depth across those countries. This proxy measures whether access exists, not how much of it is used.

Composite construction

Formula: Composite = 0.55 × hf_download_share + 0.45 × cloud_footprint_share

By construction, US composite + China composite = 100. The HF proxy is weighted higher (55%) because it provides a live, high-frequency signal of actual global model adoption. Cloud footprint (45%) is more structural and slower-moving but captures the infrastructure layer through which AI services are delivered internationally.

Update cadence

The HF downloads proxy is fetched daily via GitHub Actions — HF download figures update continuously and reflect the trailing 30-day window. The cloud footprint proxy is curated and reviewed annually; values in scripts/fetch_diffusion.py are updated manually when providers announce significant geographic expansion.

What this composite does NOT capture

  • Domestic AI adoption within each country’s home market (see Adoption dimension)
  • Chinese model downloads via ModelScope, Gitee AI, or other domestic platforms
  • Closed/proprietary model API usage (GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini, Qianwen API)
  • AI hardware exports (chips, servers) as a diffusion vector
  • Influence via joint ventures, technical assistance, or standards bodies
  • Quality or depth of AI integration in foreign deployments
  • Revenue or economic impact of diffusion

Energy Energy & Power Constraints — AI Scaling Capacity (v2)

What this measures

How well the U.S. and China can sustain large-scale AI compute growth from an energy and power infrastructure perspective. This is not a measure of total electricity generation or consumption. It focuses on four AI-relevant factors:

  • The pace of electricity supply expansion (can new capacity be built fast enough to meet AI demand?)
  • Available headroom in the grid before AI/data center demand becomes a binding constraint
  • The speed at which new power capacity can actually reach data center operators
  • The cost and access environment for AI data center operators (electricity pricing, DC siting support)

A country may have vast total electricity capacity but still face AI-specific bottlenecks if grid connection is slow, permitting is blocked, data center demand is already saturating the grid, or electricity prices make large-scale AI compute economically infeasible.

Important caveat — intermittency of new capacity: The majority of new electricity capacity in both countries is variable renewable (solar and wind). In 2024, roughly 80% of US additions and >95% of China’s additions were variable renewable. AI data centers require firm, continuous 24/7 power; solar and wind provide power only when conditions are favorable. The raw capacity addition rate therefore slightly overstates AI-usable capacity expansion. Battery storage deployment is increasing but does not yet provide multi-day firm capacity at scale. This limitation is noted but not adjusted in the normalized score — annual addition rate remains the best available signal for long-run energy supply expansion.

Proxy 1 — Electricity Capacity Addition Rate (30% weight)

Sources: U.S. EIA, Electric Power Monthly (February 2025); IEA, Renewables 2024 (November 2024); China National Energy Administration (NEA) 2024 Annual Report (国家能源局2024年能源工作综述).

Definition: Annual additions to installed electricity generation capacity, expressed as a percentage of the existing installed base. Updated to 2024 data (from 2023 in the previous version).

Values used (2024): U.S.: ~4.8% (~60 GW added; ~1,250 GW base. Solar ~35 GW, battery storage ~18 GW, wind ~7 GW). China: ~13.1% (~380 GW added; ~2,900 GW base. Record solar year: ≥280 GW solar, ~80 GW wind. China installed more solar in 2024 than the rest of the world combined.)

Normalization: (rate ÷ 15%) × 100, capped at 100.

Weight reduced from 40% to 30% compared to v1, to partially reflect the intermittency caveat above. Raw addition rate slightly overstates near-term AI-usable expansion; the weight reduction moderates the influence of this proxy without eliminating it.

Proxy 2 — Data Center Power Demand Headroom (25% weight)

Sources: IEA, Energy and AI (January 2025); IEA, Electricity 2025 (January 2025); EIA (US generation total); NBS China Statistical Yearbook 2024 (中国统计年鉴2024). Updated to 2024 estimates.

Definition: Data center electricity consumption as a percentage of total national electricity generation, inverted to a headroom score. Lower DC share = more capacity available for future AI scaling = higher score.

Values used (2024 est.): U.S.: ~6.2% DC share (~260 TWh / ~4,200 TWh) → headroom score 38.0. China: ~2.6% DC share (~250 TWh / ~9,500 TWh) → headroom score 74.0.

Normalization: Headroom score = (10 − dc_share%) ÷ 10 × 100. At 10% DC share, headroom = 0 (fully pressured).

Asymmetric trajectory: The US DC share rose from ~4.8% (2023) to ~6.2% (2024) — a significant one-year jump driven by the AI data center buildout. IEA projects US DC demand could exceed 500 TWh by 2030 under a high-AI scenario (~12% of generation), which would fully exhaust current headroom. China’s very large generation base (~9,500 TWh) provides substantially more buffer at the same absolute DC demand level.

Confidence note — China: China’s data center electricity statistics are less granular than US figures. The ~250 TWh estimate from IEA covers ICT workloads broadly; AI-specific demand is a subset. Confidence: medium. The US figure is high confidence (IEA + EIA corroboration).

Proxy 3 — Grid Connection Speed Score (25% weight)

Sources: Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), Queued Up 2025; IEA, Energy and AI (January 2025); China NDRC/NEA.

Definition: A curated 0–100 score reflecting how quickly new electricity generation capacity can be connected to the grid and made available to data center operators. Combines interconnection queue depth, regulatory speed, and national capacity for infrastructure direction.

Values used: U.S.: 30/100 — LBNL Queued Up 2025: 2,600+ GW in queues; median wait >5 years. FERC Order 2023 cluster-based reforms are underway but the backlog remains severe. Virginia and Texas AI data center clusters are already straining local grid capacity. China: 65/100 — NDRC/NEA state-directed permitting enables compressed timelines for strategic AI infrastructure. The East Data West Compute (东数西算, EDWC) program pre-approves sites and grid connections at 8 national computing power hub nodes. Score moderated by: (a) renewable curtailment in western provinces (Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang) means data centers sited for cheap power may face reliability issues; (b) west-to-east transmission gap partially addressed by Ultra-High-Voltage (UHV) buildout.

Confidence note: Grid connection speed is a curated composite assessment, not a single directly measured metric. Confidence: medium for both countries.

Proxy 4 — Energy Cost & DC Power Access (20% weight)

Sources: EIA Annual Energy Outlook 2025 (US industrial electricity prices); China NBS Statistical Yearbook 2024 / 中国统计年鉴2024 (China national electricity prices); NDRC East Data West Compute policy documents 2022–2024.

Definition: A curated 0–100 score combining three sub-factors: (a) industrial electricity price (lower = better AI ROI on compute), (b) PPA market depth (availability of long-term Power Purchase Agreements at favorable rates), (c) government and utility support for AI data center siting and preferential power pricing.

Values used: U.S.: 50/100 — Industrial electricity ~7.7 cents/kWh avg (EIA 2024), rising 8% year-over-year as AI demand increases. PPA market is deep: hyperscalers execute multi-GW contracts at 3.5–6 cents/kWh in best markets (Pacific NW, Texas, Midwest). IRA clean energy credits improve economics for large operators. Rising grid connection fees and energy price competition in key markets (Northern Virginia, Texas) offset the PPA advantage. China: 65/100 — National industrial electricity ~7.5 cents/kWh (comparable to US). Significant cost advantage in NDRC-designated AI computing zones: Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Guizhou, Sichuan offer ~2–4 cents/kWh to qualifying computing facilities. Government-directed 东数西算 (East Data West Compute) policy provides structured access to cheap western power for eastern AI workloads. Offset by: west-to-east transmission costs; renewable curtailment in cheapest zones.

Confidence note: Retail/industrial prices differ from actual contracted rates for large operators; PPA terms are commercially sensitive; Chinese zone pricing requires NDRC qualification. Confidence: medium for both countries.

Supplementary — AI Data Center Pipeline (not scored)

Announced and under-construction AI data center capacity (2024–2026 pipeline) is included in the raw data as context only — it is not used in the composite score. Reason: announced capacity frequently diverges from what is actually built on schedule; grid connection timelines mean announced capacity and available power can diverge by years; and comparing private commercial commitments (US hyperscalers) with government policy targets (China NDRC) is methodologically inconsistent.

Indicative estimates: US ~60–80 GW equivalent (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, combined CapEx exceeding $300B for 2025). China ~30–50 GW equivalent (Alibaba, ByteDance, Tencent, Huawei Cloud + EDWC national nodes; NDRC 10M rack target).

Composite construction

Formula: Composite = 0.30 × capacity_score + 0.25 × headroom_score + 0.25 × grid_score + 0.20 × energy_cost_score

Capacity addition rate (30%) measures long-run supply expansion. DC demand headroom (25%) captures near-term grid slack for additional AI load. Grid connection speed (25%) captures whether capacity can reach data centers quickly. Energy cost & access (20%) captures the economic feasibility of scaling AI compute.

Result (2024 data): US composite ~36.6, China ~73.9. China’s strong lead reflects: substantially faster capacity addition, more grid headroom relative to its large generation base, faster state-directed permitting, and preferential power pricing in EDWC zones. The US’s primary constraint is grid interconnection backlog; its PPA market provides some cost mitigation.

Update cadence

The script runs weekly via GitHub Actions (Monday 11:00 UTC). Underlying data updates annually: EIA Electric Power Monthly (~February, US capacity additions); IEA Renewables (~November, global capacity); China NEA Annual Report (~January); IEA Energy and AI (irregular, data center demand); LBNL Queued Up (~April–May); EIA Annual Energy Outlook (~February); NBS Statistical Yearbook (~October). When new editions are published, update reference values in scripts/fetch_energy.py.

What this composite does NOT capture

  • Total electricity generation or consumption (not an AI-relevant measure on its own)
  • Capacity factor / firmness of power — solar/wind additions provide fewer AI-usable MWh than nameplate MW implies
  • Carbon intensity or renewable mix of AI-relevant power
  • Nuclear power expansion (US SMR pipeline; China has 23+ units under construction as of 2024)
  • Private or off-grid power arrangements (natural gas generators co-located with AI campuses)
  • Water availability constraints for data center cooling (increasingly binding in arid US SW)
  • Long-run transmission adequacy (UHV buildout pace in China; US long-distance HVDC projects)
  • Actual PPA prices for specific hyperscaler contracts (commercially sensitive)
  • Regional grid reliability scores (NERC region data for US; provincial reliability in China)

Frontier Models Open Model Ecosystem Index — Three-Proxy Composite

What this measures

A three-proxy composite measuring U.S. and Chinese AI model activity across public release output, benchmark performance on independent evaluations, and cross-platform ecosystem distribution. Scored as a share of combined U.S. + China activity, so scores sum to 100.

Critical editorial constraint: This composite measures open-model activity and public benchmark performance. It does not measure closed-model capability. The most capable U.S. models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.7, Gemini 2.5) and Chinese models (Qwen API, Doubao, Hunyuan) are closed and not fully reflected here. The score is best interpreted as a measure of lab output velocity and open-ecosystem reach, not a definitive ranking of national frontier AI capability.

Why move beyond Hugging Face Hub alone?

The previous version of this dimension counted model update activity on Hugging Face Hub only. This created a systematic undercount of China for two reasons:

  • Platform bias: HuggingFace Hub is the dominant global open-source platform, but Chinese AI labs also distribute widely through ModelScope (魔搭社区, operated by Alibaba), Gitee AI, and Baidu AI Studio. Labs that publish primarily to domestic platforms are invisible in HF-only counts.
  • Activity ≠ capability: Model update counts on HF measure lab output velocity, not benchmark performance. A lab releasing many fine-tuned variants scores high; a lab releasing one highly capable model scores low.

The new composite addresses both issues: it adds a ModelScope supplement for Chinese labs with limited HF presence, adds a benchmark performance proxy, and adds an ecosystem breadth proxy that explicitly weights cross-platform distribution.

Proxy 1 — Release Activity (35% weight)

Sources: Hugging Face Hub API (automated, daily); ModelScope supplement (manual estimate in data/frontier_models_manual.json).

Definition: Count of model versions published by tracked labs (see data/labs.json) in the rolling 30-day window on Hugging Face Hub, plus an estimated supplement for Chinese labs that publish primarily on ModelScope and are not fully tracked via HF.

Tracked labs: 31 labs as of April 2026 (16 US-attributed, 12 China-attributed, 1 Other, 2 additional US labs). See data/labs.json for the full list. Labs are classified by primary country of headquarters.

Coverage note — ModelScope supplement: The supplement is a conservative lower-bound estimate based on observed ModelScope activity for labs not already tracked via HF (BAAI, Baichuan Intelligence, Kuaishou AI, Sensetime, Vivo AI/BlueLM). Labs already tracked via HF (Qwen, DeepSeek, THUDM, etc.) are not double-counted. Confidence: Low. A proper ModelScope API integration would replace this manual supplement in a future version.

Coverage note — Gitee AI and Baidu AI Studio: These platforms are not currently tracked. Gitee AI is China’s open-source code platform with a growing model repository; Baidu AI Studio hosts PaddlePaddle-based models. Integrating these sources would further reduce the China undercount.

Proxy 2 — Benchmark Performance (45% weight)

Sources: LMSYS Chatbot Arena Elo leaderboard (manual snapshot); Epoch AI Notable AI Models dataset (manual snapshot). Both stored in data/frontier_models_manual.json.

Definition: Average of two sub-proxies, equally weighted (50% each):

  • LMSYS Chatbot Arena top-20 share: Count of U.S.- and China-attributed models in the top 20 by Elo rating, scored as (US / US+China) × 100. Arena Elo is the most widely cited independent third-party benchmark for general instruction-following capability.
  • Epoch AI notable models (2-year window) share: Count of notable AI models since a rolling 2-year cutoff, attributed to U.S. or Chinese labs. Epoch AI applies a documented selection methodology based on training compute, benchmark performance, and research significance.

Why benchmark performance gets the highest weight (45%): It is the most analytically defensible proxy for the dimension’s intended concept — how capable are each country’s AI models? Release activity counts alone conflate volume with quality.

Limitations: Arena Elo only covers models submitted for public human evaluation. Some frontier models (e.g., internal enterprise API variants) may not be submitted. Chinese models relying on domestic-only APIs may be structurally underrepresented in Arena despite comparable real-world capability. Epoch AI’s selection criteria, while documented, may systematically exclude Chinese lab models with limited English documentation. Both sources are updated by manual snapshot in this tracker — they do not auto-update daily.

Proxy 3 — Ecosystem Breadth (20% weight)

Sources: Hugging Face Hub API (monthly downloads, automated); ModelScope platform presence (manual estimate). Both in data/frontier_models_manual.json.

Definition: A platform-weighted distribution score capturing how broadly each country’s open models are used across the two largest model platforms globally: HuggingFace Hub and ModelScope. Scored as (US share / US+China) × 100.

Formula: Ecosystem score = 0.55 × HF_download_share + 0.45 × ModelScope_presence_share

Key result: China leads on ecosystem breadth when ModelScope is included, because Chinese labs dominate ModelScope (the second-largest global model platform) even as U.S. models dominate HuggingFace downloads. This is analytically important: a HF-only view structurally undercounts the reach of China’s open AI ecosystem.

Confidence note — ModelScope presence: The ModelScope share is a manual estimate based on the top-1000 model list composition. Confidence: Low. HF weighted at 55% (global reach, higher confidence) vs ModelScope at 45% (China-primary, lower confidence). A direct ModelScope API integration would improve this.

Composite construction

Formula: Composite = 0.35 × release_activity_share + 0.45 × benchmark_share + 0.20 × ecosystem_share

By construction, US composite + China composite = 100. The executive summary score is composite / 10, giving a 0–10 scale where US + China = 10.

Benchmark performance is weighted highest (45%) because it most directly measures the dimension’s intended concept. Release activity (35%) captures lab output velocity and is the most automatable proxy. Ecosystem breadth (20%) captures platform distribution but relies partly on manual estimates.

Labs tracked and country classification

Labs are classified by the country of the primary organization’s headquarters. The full list is in data/labs.json. Notable inclusions and edge cases:

  • Google / DeepMind: Classified as US. DeepMind is UK-based but Google (US) is the parent organization.
  • Cohere: Classified as US. Incorporated in Canada/US, primarily US-funded frontier model lab.
  • Mistral AI: Classified as Other (France). The only non-US, non-China lab tracked.
  • NVIDIA: Classified as US. Note that NVIDIA publishes models across many specialized domains (robotics, medical imaging, weather) and re-hosts some third-party weights; its raw count may not reflect general-purpose frontier capability.
  • BAAI (Beijing Academy of AI): Classified as China. State-backed research institute; publishes FlagAI, FlagEmbedding, AquilaChat.
  • Baichuan Intelligence: Classified as China. Strong ModelScope presence alongside HF.

What this composite does NOT capture

  • Closed-model capability: GPT-4o, Claude, Gemini Ultra, Qwen API, Doubao, Hunyuan
  • Government or defense AI development programs (non-public)
  • Gitee AI, Baidu AI Studio, or other Chinese platforms (not yet integrated)
  • Model quality or downstream task performance beyond Arena Elo
  • Multimodal capability, embodied AI, or specialized domain models
  • Lab research output not yet released as a public model

Update cadence

Release activity (HF Hub) is fetched daily via GitHub Actions. Benchmark snapshots (Arena, Epoch AI) are manually updated in data/frontier_models_manual.json when new data is available — approximately monthly for Arena and quarterly for Epoch AI. ModelScope supplement and ecosystem breadth estimates are reviewed quarterly.

Compute Triangulated Compute Index

The compute dimension uses a triangulated index combining three complementary proxies. No single source has complete visibility into AI compute — each proxy fills different blind spots. The composite score always represents US share of combined US+China compute.

Why triangulation is necessary

Three systematic gaps prevent any single source from being reliable:

  1. China’s exascale HPC: China stopped submitting most major supercomputers to TOP500 after 2023. The November 2025 list shows 40 Chinese systems at ~205 PFlops — analysts estimate China’s actual capacity is 10× higher.
  2. Private AI clusters: xAI Colossus, Meta’s AI datacenters, and ByteDance clusters are not in TOP500 and not publicly benchmarked.
  3. Domestic Chinese chips: Huawei Ascend deployment scale is not officially reported — the adjustment relies on analyst estimates and procurement data.

Proxy 1 — Training Compute (weight 40%)

Source: Epoch AI “Notable AI Models” CSV, models published since 2023 with disclosed training compute ≥1020 FLOPs. US labs hold ~85% of disclosed training FLOPs; China ~15%.

Known undercount for China: Frontier closed models — Qwen-Max, Doubao, Hunyuan, Gemini (Google) — do not publish training compute. DeepSeek R1 and Qwen-open are included; most ByteDance and Baidu frontier models are not. The 85/15 split is a lower bound for how much China has invested in training.

Proxy 2 — Hardware Supply (weight 40%)

Measures the flow of AI compute hardware to each country, not just disclosed training runs. Two inputs:

  • NVIDIA geographic data center revenue (FY2025 10-K): US customers (AWS, Azure, Google, Meta) received 47% of NVIDIA’s $115B data center revenue ($54.1B). China received 13% ($15.0B), constrained by A100/H100 export controls since Oct 2023. Confidence: high (audited SEC filing).
  • Huawei Ascend adjustment: ~1.5M Ascend 910B chips deployed (analyst estimates). At 35% H100 efficiency (640 vs. 1,979 TFLOPS BF16), this equals ~525K H100-equivalents or ~$10.5B in hardware value. Adding this brings China’s hardware supply share to ~32%. Confidence: low — not officially confirmed; treat as order-of-magnitude.

Adjusted totals: US 68%, China 32%.

Proxy 3 — Visible HPC (weight 20%)

Source: TOP500 list (updated June/November) plus manually maintained corrections for systems not submitted.

  • US: TOP500 raw (6,961 PFlops) + private AI cluster estimates (xAI Colossus ~600, Meta ~900, Microsoft ~800 PFlops = +2,300) = 9,261 PFlops adjusted.
  • China: TOP500 raw (205 PFlops) + non-submission corrections (Tianhe-3 ~1,400, Sunway NG ~1,000, Beijing AI Center ~400, EDWC aggregate ~500 = +3,300) = 3,505 PFlops adjusted.

Adjusted totals: US 72.5%, China 27.5%. Uncertainty range: China 24–32%.

Composite and hidden-compute band

The composite is a weighted average of the three share scores: 0.40 × training compute + 0.40 × hardware supply + 0.20 × visible HPC ≈ US 75.7%, China 24.3%. Converted to the 0–10 scale: approximately US 7.6, China 2.4.

A separate hidden-compute uncertainty band is displayed in the UI but is not used in scoring. Expert synthesis incorporating all three gaps suggests China’s true share of combined US+China compute capacity is likely 25–40% (point estimate 30%). The scored composite (~25%) is a conservative lower bound reflecting the systematic non-reporting described above.

Manual inputs (hardware supply adjustments, HPC corrections) are maintained in data/compute_manual.json and should be updated when new NVIDIA 10-K filings, Huawei product announcements, or HPC disclosures become available.

Talent AI Talent Pipeline Index

A multi-source index combining three complementary views of the talent competition: where AI research is being produced (research output), where researchers are being trained (domestic pipeline), and where the world’s best AI researchers actually work (elite concentration). Each captures a different part of the talent story — none alone is sufficient.

Why a single metric is insufficient

The talent competition is layered, and different metrics tell very different stories:

  • Paper volume alone puts China at ~64% of the US+China total and implies a large China lead. This is real — China produces far more AI research papers — but paper volume does not measure where elite researchers work, or which papers drive frontier AI systems.
  • Elite researcher placement alone puts the US at ~72% and implies a large US lead. This is also real — US frontier labs concentrate a disproportionate share of the world’s top AI researchers — but it ignores China’s massive production pipeline that supplies global AI talent.
  • PhD pipeline alone implies near-total China dominance (~81%), but many China-trained researchers work in the US or Europe after graduation.

The composite deliberately balances these perspectives, yielding a near-tie (≈50/50) that reflects the genuine complexity of the talent competition.

Proxy 1 — Research Output Quality (weight 35%)

Source: OpenAlex API. Three sub-signals, each counted by institution country of at least one author:

  • Paper volume (30% within proxy): AI/ML/NLP/CV concept papers published in the last 12 months. Includes all indexed venues. China leads substantially (~64% share). Papers with multinational authorship are counted in each country — the sum of country counts exceeds the paper total. Concepts: Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, NLP, Computer Vision (OpenAlex concept IDs).
  • High-impact papers (40% within proxy): Papers published in the last 3 years with ≥50 citations. Filters to work with demonstrated impact beyond volume. US leads (~54% share). Note: cited_by_count is the current total, not a 3-year count — newer papers are disadvantaged. OpenAlex filter may not be available on all instances; seeded estimates are used as fallback.
  • Top-cited papers (30% within proxy): Papers published in the last 2 years with ≥25 citations. Intermediate quality threshold. US slight lead (~52% share). Same fallback caveat as high-impact.

Key bias: OpenAlex counts affiliation at time of publication, not nationality. A China-born researcher at MIT is counted as a US author. This inflates the US share in all OpenAlex metrics — the true “Chinese-origin” research contribution is higher than country-of-institution counting shows.

Chinese-language sources: Chinese researchers increasingly publish in Chinese-language journals and conference proceedings not indexed in OpenAlex (e.g., 计算机学报 (Journal of Computer Science), 中国科学：信息科学 (SCIENCE CHINA Information Sciences), CNKI preprints). This systematically undercounts China’s true research output volume in OpenAlex. The 64% paper-volume share for China is likely an undercount.

Proxy 2 — Domestic Talent Pipeline (weight 25%)

Sources: NSF Survey of Earned Doctorates 2022 (US) and China Ministry of Education statistics 2022 (China, 教育部统计数据).

  • US: ~10,477 CS doctoral degrees awarded in 2022 (NSF SED). Estimated 35% AI-adjacent = ~3,667 AI-related PhDs. ~66% of US CS PhD recipients are foreign-born; ~28% are China-born. The metric counts graduates at US universities regardless of nationality. Confidence: high (government survey, comprehensive).
  • China: ~52,000 CS/IT doctoral degrees awarded in 2022 (MoE). Estimated 30% AI-adjacent = ~15,600 AI-related PhDs. China’s broad CS classification includes applied engineering not classified as AI research in Western databases; the adjusted figure is conservative. ~12% pursue graduate training abroad, primarily in the US. Confidence: medium (official statistics, but AI classification is broader and peer-reviewed verification is limited).

China produces approximately 4× more AI-adjacent PhDs per year. China share: ~81%; US share: ~19%. This is a flow metric (graduates per year), not a stock metric (total active researchers). It measures long-run production capacity of the university system.

Proxy 3 — Elite Researcher Concentration + Migration (weight 40%)

Source: MacroPolo AI Talent Tracker 2023, covering the institutional affiliations of the top 2,000 AI researchers by citation count at NeurIPS, ICML, and ICLR.

Key findings used in this proxy:

  • ~57% of the world’s top AI researchers work at US institutions. ~22% work at Chinese institutions. US share of US+China combined: ~72.2%. Confidence: medium-high (systematic methodology on public data; ~1-2 year lag).
  • Migration pattern: ~36% of China-undergraduate AI researchers in the global top-tier work at US institutions. ~34% work at Chinese institutions (up from ~25% in 2019). This is the primary talent flow: China trains the pipeline, the US captures much of the elite, but China’s domestic retention is rising.
  • US frontier lab concentration: OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta AI, and Microsoft Research collectively hold the largest concentrations of top-cited AI researchers globally. Chinese counterparts (BAAI, Shanghai AI Lab, ByteDance Research) are growing but smaller in absolute scale.

This proxy receives the highest weight (40%) because placement at frontier labs has the most direct connection to producing breakthrough AI systems. The US advantage here is real and large, but China’s trajectory is important: domestic retention has improved significantly, and Chinese frontier labs have become serious research institutions.

Composite and interpretation

The composite (weights 35/25/40) produces a near-tie: US ~50%, China ~50%. This is a meaningful result, not a rounding artifact. It reflects a genuine structural tension: China leads strongly on pipeline volume (proxy 2), and leads slightly on research output quantity (proxy 1); the US leads substantially on elite researcher capture (proxy 3). These effects nearly cancel out at the composite level.

The key policy-relevant insight is not “who is winning” but the underlying dynamic: the US benefits disproportionately from Chinese-trained researchers, and if US-China talent flows were severed or China continued accelerating domestic retention, China’s composite score would rise substantially.
